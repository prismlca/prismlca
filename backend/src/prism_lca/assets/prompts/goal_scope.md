Phrase the goal and scope of this study as continuous prose, from the recorded metadata.
The metadata fields are working notes, not finished sentences — that is deliberate, and turning them into readable prose is your job. Cover the goal, the intended audience, the functional unit, the system boundaries, the cut-off criteria, and both allocation decisions: process allocation (how a multi-output process is split) and system allocation (how end-of-life credits and burdens are handled). Name each decision and what it means for the results.
Every statement must come from that metadata or from what you look up — a dataset's own description (`get_process_details`), a parameter and its note (`list_parameters`). Add nothing that is in neither, and if a field is empty, say that it has not been defined rather than filling the gap.
Your prose *replaces* the fields in the document; they are not printed beside it. So write the chapter, not a tidied copy of the notes: bullet points become sentences, the order that reads best becomes the order, and something recorded twice is said once.
A field may also hold something that is not a scope statement at all — an instruction to an assistant (“use web research for …”, “research or estimate the masses and store them as parameters”), a leftover, a note to self. Skip it without a word: it belongs to how the model was built, not to what the study assessed, and a sentence about it tells the reader about the tooling. Only when a field holds nothing else — no usable statement remains once the instruction is set aside — say that this part of the scope has not been defined, in one plain sentence that does not quote the instruction. A study whose scope is undefined has to look undefined, and a smooth sentence covering the gap is the one outcome worse than the gap.
**Optional but expected where the data allows it, as published product LCAs do: the reference flow beside the functional unit, and what the data covers.** The functional unit is the function; the reference flow is the quantity that delivers it — “one square metre coated” against “0.196 kg of stain plus 0.8 containers”. For coverage, say only what you can see: the geography the study is set in, the background database and its version, and where a dataset's own geography differs from the study's. **A year is quoted, never inferred.** Name one only where you have read it — in a process description, or in a dataset you opened — and attach it to the thing it actually belongs to, said out loud: the data sheet the formulation comes from, the statistic behind a recycling rate, the transport shares inside a market. What does not exist anywhere in front of you is a reference year for the **study**, the **inventory** or the **background database as a whole**, and a year lifted out of one dataset's comment is not one: a market whose comment dates its transport statistics says nothing about when the production behind it was measured. Where no year is recorded, say so — a plausible one is the most quotable wrong sentence a report can contain.
Two things real studies always make concrete, and a reader misses at once when they are missing. **The cut-off is named with its examples**: not “below one percent by mass” alone but what actually fell below it — the label, the tin coating, the pigments — because a threshold without its list cannot be judged. And **the functional unit is justified, not just stated**: say what function it fixes and, where the study compares, what had to be set aside to make two things comparable at all. Both only from what the metadata records.
Name a field the way the reader knows it — "the goal", "the scope", "the cut-off criteria" — never by its key. A real report came out saying "a portion of the `study.goal` field contains an instruction": the reader has no `study.goal`, they have a study, and an internal path in a client's document reads as something that leaked. For the same reason, no backticks: the document sets them literally, so they print as backticks.
