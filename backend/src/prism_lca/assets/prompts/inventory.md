Write the life cycle inventory chapter: what was modelled, and how far its data can be trusted. It stands under a table of the foreground processes and is the chapter's body — not a remark about it.
Each process carries a note under `foreground`, written by whoever built the model. Those notes are **working notes, not prose**: shorthand, half-sentences, a source pasted after a dash. They are your material and they are **not printed** — the reader sees only what you write, so anything the report should say has to be said by you, in full sentences. The reasons recorded on single inputs sit under `exchange_notes`, by process and input — why this dataset and not its equivalent, why a proxy geography, why a waste flow has its sign. Working notes too, and the place a proxy's reason is most often found.
- **Describe the processes.** Go through the foreground the way the table orders it, beginning with the reference process, and say for each what it represents and what it is made of. Related processes belong in one paragraph — five packaging steps are a paragraph about packaging, not five paragraphs. Name a process the way the table names it, so the reader can find it.
- **Where the data comes from.** Say plainly which parts rest on measured or primary data, which on a background dataset, and which on an estimate. The notes and the parameter comments record this, and several say so in as many words.
- **Where a proxy was used and what it costs.** A dataset from another country or another technology standing in for this one is a normal modelling decision and a reportable one: name the substitution and the direction of its likely bias, from what the note records.
- **What is not in the model**, where the notes say so.
- **Optional, and worth it in a model with more than a handful of background links:** group them by what they supply — materials, energy, transport, treatment — instead of walking the table. Published inventories are organised that way because a reader looks for one kind at a time, and it costs you nothing you do not already have.
- **Bound a data gap instead of only naming it.** This is what separates a report from a disclaimer, and real studies do it as a matter of course: where a generic dataset stands in for something small, say what an error would cost. The shares you are given are shares of the IMPACT, never of the mass — `contribution.groups.0.share_text` is what the largest group contributes to the result: “the undeclared remainder sits outside the group that carries {{contribution.groups.0.share_text}} of the result; even an order-of-magnitude error in it would move the total by less than the spread between the scenarios”. Use only shares you were given; if the arithmetic is not in front of you, say the gap is unbounded and why — that is also a finding.
- **Say what a proxy was ADJUSTED for, not only that it is one.** A European dataset standing in for another region is a proxy plus a decision: which electricity mix, which transport distance, which reference year was kept and which was changed. Where the note records the adjustment, name it; where it records none, say the dataset was taken unchanged — the reader cannot tell those two apart from the dataset name.
**Open the background datasets you are about to name.** This is the chapter where a lookup pays for itself most: a note says which dataset stands in, and `get_process_details` says what that dataset *is* — an average of what, covering which step, from which supplier. Naming a dataset you cannot describe leaves the reader with a string.
**A “market for …” dataset is worth opening every time.** A market is a consumption mix, not a producer: its exchanges name the production datasets behind it, their geographies and their shares (call it with `summary=true` and read `biggest_inputs`). That is how a sentence becomes checkable — “the European market draws its entire volume from a single production dataset” says something; “market for X (RER)” says nothing. Where one supplier carries the whole market, say so: it means the result rests on that one dataset.
- Close on the pattern across the processes: how much of this study is its own data, and where the weak points are.
Write it as the study's author would: continuous prose, no bullet list of processes, and nothing invented that the notes do not carry. A note that says nothing usable is said to say nothing — never filled in. No results here; this chapter is about the inventory, not about what it scores.
