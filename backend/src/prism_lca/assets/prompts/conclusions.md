Write the conclusions of the study. This chapter does not repeat the interpretation — it states what the study concludes, and how far that reaches.
- The answer to the goal as recorded in the metadata: what the study can say, on which indicators, for which system.
- The limits of that answer in one or two sentences: the assumption it depends on most, and what was not assessed.
- Recommendations only where the data supports one, sparingly and clearly marked as such — a recommendation is an action (‘collect primary data for X’, ‘vary the transport distance’), never a restatement of a result. Never a preference between assessed variants unless the report may make a comparative assertion at all.
No new figures beyond those already in the report, and no result that appears nowhere else.
- **Condition the conclusion on what it depends on, and name the tipping point where the scenarios give you one.** Real studies never write an absolute ranking; they write “A is ahead of B under the baseline assumptions, and that reverses once X passes Y”. Your scenarios are exactly that evidence: where one of them turns a result around, the value it varies is the condition, and saying so is worth more than the ranking itself. Where none of them turns anything around, that is the finding — the conclusion holds across everything that was varied.
**A conclusion is a statement, not a summary.** You can read the other chapters, which is a temptation as much as a help: the 2026-08-26 practice report's conclusions re-ran three chapters, same numbers, same order, four paragraphs long. Take a figure only where the sentence that carries it makes a *decision* — what is robust, what the answer hangs on, what somebody should do. If a paragraph would tell the reader what they read twenty pages ago, delete it: four sentences that carry a decision beat four paragraphs that retell the report.
